### Architecture

Both networks share the identical **Fairy-Stockfish variant-nnue-pytorch** architecture:

- **FT**: 45,056 × 512 (feature transformer)

- **L1**: 1,024 × 16 (first hidden layer, 8 layer stacks)

- **L2**: 16 × 32 (second hidden layer)

- **Out**: 32 × 1 (output)

- **PSQT buckets**: 8

---

- **FT Weights** Observed: Atomic mean −9.6 vs Chess −4.1; sparsity drops ~31 % → ~22 %.

- **PSQT Weights** Observed: mean flips +71.5 → −227.2; standard deviation reduces substantially.

- **L1 Biases** Observed: mean −1010.7 → +369.7 (+1380 shift).

- **L2 Weights** Observed: sparsity increases ~20 % → ~29 %; mean shifts +2.1 → −1.8.

- **Output Bias** Observed: int16‑domain bias −68 vs +745 (813 offset).

### FT Weights Histogram

<img src="file:///D:/develop/chess/logs/analysis/analysis_0/nnue_analysis/hist_FT_Weights.png" title="" alt="hist_FT_Weights.png" data-align="center">

- Chess: Extremely sharp, narrow peak at ~0 with heavy tails. The distribution is leptokurtic — most weights are near zero with a few strong outliers. This is classic for a well‑regularized network: most raw board features are weakly relevant, a few are strongly relevant.

- Atomic: The peak is broader and flatter — weights are more spread out. The overlay shows atomic has fatter tails and less concentration at exactly zero. This matches the measured sparsity drop from ~31 % down to ~22 % on FT weights: atomic uses more of its raw feature space because atomic chess positions have more distinct piece‑interaction patterns that need encoding. In standard chess, many piece configurations are "quiet", and their corresponding FT weights get pushed near zero during training, leading to small contributions to FT activations. In atomic, almost every piece placement has explosive implications, so fewer raw features can be safely down‑weighted toward zero.

### PSQT Weights

<img src="file:///D:/develop/chess/logs/analysis/analysis_0/nnue_analysis/hist_PSQT_Weights.png" title="" alt="hist_PSQT_Weights.png" data-align="center">

This is the most striking distributional difference across all layers. The chess PSQT forms a multi‑modal distribution with prominent peaks near −10,000, 0, and +15,000. Classically, these peaks map to: negative values for unfavourable squares such as edges and corners, near‑zero values for neutral squares, and positive values for strong central or outpost squares.

The atomic PSQT, by contrast, is unimodal, roughly Gaussian, and centred close to zero; the large separated extreme peaks disappear. The overlay shows minimal overlap in the far tails, highlighting a fundamental reshaping of the PSQT table.

This aligns with atomic chess rules: static square quality becomes far less important than explosive piece interaction geometry. A rim knight may set up a destructive detonating fork, while a centralised queen can be catastrophic if adjacent to enemy pieces and vulnerable to a capture chain reaction. Since PSQT only encodes independent piece‑square lookup and cannot represent pairwise explosive interactions, the network shifts the heavy lifting of evaluation to the feature transformer and hidden layers. With a mean near zero (+71.5 for chess vs −227.2 for atomic) and reduced standard deviation, the atomic PSQT functions more as a small correction term rather than the primary evaluation signal seen in standard chess.

### L1 Weights

<img src="file:///D:/develop/chess/logs/analysis/analysis_0/nnue_analysis/hist_L1_Weights.png" title="" alt="hist_L1_Weights.png" data-align="center">

Chess L1 weights span a broad range roughly from −100 to +100 with a moderate peak around zero. The atomic L1 distribution forms a far sharper peak centered near zero and falls off quickly away from the midpoint. Atomic also shows higher L1 sparsity (45% vs 37%), which at first glance appears to conflict with the reduced sparsity observed in FT weights.

The apparent conflict resolves when viewing the network as a two-stage pipeline: the L1 layer accepts the concatenated 1024-dimensional accumulator output from FT. A sparser L1 means atomic’s first hidden layer is more selective about which accumulator dimensions contribute to its internal representation. The FT expands to encode more atomic-specific raw features, while L1 aggressively filters this richer representation down to a smaller subset useful for predicting game outcomes.

The pronounced peak near zero may point toward stronger regularization during atomic training, or reflect that the task naturally encourages the model to discard irrelevant accumulator dimensions; this cannot be confirmed from the weight histogram alone.

### L1 Biases

<img src="file:///D:/develop/chess/logs/analysis/analysis_0/nnue_analysis/hist_L1_Biases.png" title="" alt="hist_L1_Biases.png" data-align="center">

Chess L1 biases span a wide dynamic range from −11,907 to +8,796, with values distributed broadly across the full interval. Atomic L1 biases are compressed into a narrower band, approximately −6,714 to +6,644, and the distribution mean shifts from −1,010 to +370.

This contrast is meaningful: chess L1 neurons possess very different baseline activations, consistent with specialization across diverse position classes such as open games, closed structures, and endgames. For atomic chess, the tighter, more uniform bias distribution aligns with the game’s consistent tactical nature — play revolves almost entirely around explosive capture threats rather than long-term static planning. As a result, the network appears to require less diversity in baseline activation across L1 neurons.

### L2 Biases

### Output Weights

<img src="file:///D:/develop/chess/logs/analysis/analysis_0/nnue_analysis/hist_Out_Weights.png" title="" alt="hist_Out_Weights.png" data-align="center">

The output layer contains only 32 weights, making this a small-sample distribution, yet meaningful contrasts emerge.
Chess output weights take discrete values around −37, −25, −13, 0, +13, +25, +37, forming an approximately symmetric distribution with visible gaps. Atomic output weights occupy a similar grid of magnitudes (−25, −13, 0, +13, +25, +37) with comparable spacing but different frequency counts for each value.

The overlay demonstrates that both models reuse many common weight magnitudes, but assign them to distinct neurons. Functionally, the output layer reweights which L2 features contribute to the final evaluation score. Given the very small parameter count of 32 weights, local adjustments in this layer can exert a substantial influence on the resulting evaluation function.
